# A Twitter support agent for Delta — and why I don't trust it yet

**Brand:** @Delta · **Golden set:** 200 hand-labelled messages · **Models:** `qwen2.5:3b-instruct` (generate), `llama3.2:3b` (judge), `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` (retrieval) — all local on a 4 GB laptop GPU.

Every figure below is generated by `tools/make_report_assets.py` from `report/metrics.json`, `report/judge_validation.json` and `report/failure_analysis.json`. None are transcribed by hand.

Reproduce in 56 seconds, no GPU or API key: `pip install -r requirements.txt && python run.py reproduce`

---

## 1. Problem framing

### What "good" means for Delta

An airline support agent isn't judged on fluency. It's judged on whether letting it reply unsupervised is *safe*. That decomposes into three questions, in increasing order of importance:

1. **Does it know what the customer wants?** — intent, measured by macro-F1 so the tail can't hide behind the head.
2. **Is the reply grounded in what Delta actually does?** — a fluent reply inventing a phone number is worse than a vague one, because it's published publicly under the brand's name.
3. **Does it know when to stop?** — escalation. The asymmetry drives the whole design: a **missed escalation** (a stranded passenger gets a canned reply) costs trust and possibly regulatory exposure; an **over-escalation** costs a few minutes of agent time. These are never averaged into one number anywhere in this report.

Operating target: **high escalation recall, accept the precision cost, auto-handle only where precedent clearly supports it.**

### What I found in the data that changed the framing

**About a third of Delta's inbound isn't a support request.** Clustering 27,168 reconstructed threads put ~13% in praise, ~14% in mixed sentiment, ~6% in photo/promo chatter. The agent's first job is triage between actionable and social — not answering.

**21.6% of Delta's replies push the customer to DMs**, so those resolutions are invisible to us. That caps how "grounded in how the brand resolved similar issues" any system here can be, before any modelling.

### What I chose not to build

- **No chat UI** — not asked for, and it would consume the evaluation's time budget.
- **No multi-turn dialogue** — the agent handles the opening message, which is what the live system receives.
- **No fine-tuning** — with 200 labels and a 4 GB GPU it would overfit, and its evaluation would be *less* trustworthy.
- **No Banking77** — retail-banking intents describe a different resolution structure; forcing it in would produce a taxonomy fitting neither dataset.
- **No vector DB** — brute-force cosine over 19,534 vectors is sub-millisecond.

### What the numbers actually say

**Escalation is where the agent earns its cost.** 6 missed vs the simple baseline's 22, and 34 for trivial. It catches 82% of cases needing a human. But it buys that by escalating **70% of all messages** against a true rate of 17% — precision 0.201. As an automation product that's close to useless; as a safety net it's the best of the four.

**The few-shot fix was the single largest intervention.** Zero-shot, the 3B classifier never emitted `booking_change_refund` or `other` at all and dumped 44% of predictions into two catch-all labels. Adding 18 exemplars — drawn only from threads absent from the golden set, with intents from clustering rather than human labels — moved `booking_change_refund` from 0.000/0.000 precision/recall to 0.370/0.417. Both runs are reported so the size of that fix is visible rather than folded into one headline.

**The trivial baseline is the metric-inflation check** and it worked: 0.200 accuracy but macro-F1 0.037 and escalation recall exactly 0.

## 3. Failure analysis

Two of the five were serious enough to fix rather than just report. The before/after numbers are both shown; `data/archive/preds_agent_before_reply_guards.jsonl` holds the unguarded outputs.

**1. Replies addressed the wrong customer — 182/200 (91%) → 0. [fixed]**
The drafter copied the anonymised handle out of retrieved precedent: `@123456 Hi, please call our customer service…`. In production every one of those would have @-mentioned a stranger. *Hypothesis:* the precedent examples carry a leading handle and the model treats it as part of the reply format. *Fix:* strip anonymised handles from precedent **before** it enters the prompt, plus an output filter as a backstop. Prevention did nearly all the work — the backstop fired only twice. Brand handles like `@Delta` are deliberately left intact.

**2. Fabricated contact details — 3 → 0. [fixed]**
`1-800-DELTA2` (twice) and `855-551-2113`, none present in the retrieved evidence; 5 other numbers *were* correctly grounded. *Hypothesis:* the model pattern-matches "support reply → give a hotline" and completes a plausible-looking number when precedent lacks one. The most dangerous behaviour in the system — fluent, confident, published under the brand's name. *Fix:* strip any phone number or URL that does not appear verbatim in the retrieved evidence.

Both guards touch reply text only: intent and escalation predictions are byte-identical across the two runs (200/200), so no headline metric moves. Note that the judge and human reply scores in §4 were collected on the **pre-guard** replies — both scored the same text, so the agreement statistic remains valid.

**3. Over-escalation is the LLM's fault, not the rules' — 100 of 111 cases.**
`decided_by` attributes it: keyword rules produced 11 over-escalations, the LLM adjudicator 100. *Hypothesis:* the triage prompt frames escalation as the safe default with no cost for choosing it. *Fix:* give the LLM the asymmetry explicitly and calibrate a threshold on held-out data.

**4. Self-reported confidence carries zero information.**
0.974 when correct, 0.975 when wrong (n=75 / n=125). I predicted this before seeing data (decision #10), which is why routing uses retrieval score rather than the model's opinion of itself. *Hypothesis:* instruction-tuned 3B models emit high confidence as a stylistic default.

**5. Intent confusions concentrate on the non-actionable classes.**
Top pairs: `praise_compliment`→`social_chatter` (6), `other`→`social_chatter` (6), `praise_compliment`→`service_complaint` (6), `booking_change_refund`→`support_unreachable` (5). *Hypothesis:* partly genuine — the praise/chatter/other boundary is fuzzy for humans too (it accounts for 7 of my own 28 re-label flips). Partly a data artefact: **12/200 messages mention a competitor airline and 7/200 never mention @Delta at all**, so "Thanks @AmericanAir for being the worse airline" is sarcasm about a different carrier that the classifier is asked to file under Delta support.

**Also observed:** a sixth of agent replies were verbatim copies of retrieved precedent — on that slice the drafter degenerates into the simple baseline.

## 4. What is misleading about my headline number?

**The headline is 0.375 accuracy. My own labels agree with themselves 0.425 of the time** (κ = 0.326, n=40, re-labelled at 12.7s per example after a delay, with pass-1 answers hidden). The model matches my labels almost as often as I match my own. At that point the metric cannot cleanly separate model error from label noise, and every comparison in Section 2 rests on ground truth of limited stability. This is the single most important caveat in the report.

**Worse: my escalation policy is not stable, and that undermines the agent's best result.** Across the same 40 examples I escalated **5 in pass 1 and 21 in pass 2** — a 4× shift, with 18 of 20 flips going auto→escalate. Raw agreement 0.500, κ = 0.036, which is chance. So "the agent misses only 6 escalations against the baseline's 22" is measured against a threshold that moves when I re-apply it. The direction of the result is probably real — the agent escalates far more aggressively than any labelling of mine — but the precise counts should not be quoted as if the target were fixed.

An earlier re-label pass run at 6.3s/example reported escalation agreement of 0.775, which looked reassuring and was not: its κ was −0.125, i.e. the agreement was entirely explained by both passes defaulting to "auto". The slower pass is worse-looking and more informative. Both are in `data/archive/`.

**What would fix this:** a second annotator, and a written escalation policy fixed *before* labelling rather than reconstructed per example. Both are in Section 5.

**The reply-quality half of this report does not exist.** The LLM judge scores κ_w = **0.003** against 44 blind human scores, with overall Spearman **−0.261**. Every axis lands in the noise band. It is also systematically lenient — it rates tone 4.75 where I rate 2.57. **No reply-quality claim is made anywhere in this report**, and the conclusions rest on intent and escalation, which don't depend on the judge. A weak-but-measured instrument is a result; an unmeasured one would have been decoration.

**"Resolution" is not observable.** `resolved_proxy` means "Delta replied publicly and didn't punt to DMs". Twitter never tells us whether the problem was fixed, and for the 21.6% punted to DMs the actual resolution happened where this dataset cannot see. Every claim about grounding in "how the brand resolved similar issues" is really grounding in *how the brand responded*.

**The golden set is deliberately not production-distributed.** Stratification over-samples rare intents and escalation-worthy cases so those metrics are measurable at all. **Aggregate accuracy here is therefore not a production estimate.** Sampling weights are retained so it could be reweighted; that reweighting has not been done.

**n=200 gives ±6pp.** Differences below that aren't evidence, which is why system comparisons use a paired bootstrap and anything crossing zero is reported as unsupported.

**One annotator wrote the labels, the prompts, and the rubric.** Self-agreement measures consistency, not correctness. A systematic misunderstanding is invisible to it, and because the same person wrote both the classifier prompt and the labels, our blind spots are correlated in the direction that flatters the system.

**Escalation labels encode my policy, not Delta's.** A real deployment would inherit that policy from the support organisation and the metrics would move with it.

**One brand, one time period, and filtering removed real traffic** — non-English, sub-20-character, and multi-customer threads are excluded from sampling but exist in production and are disproportionately hard.

## 5. What I'd do next with one more week

Ordered by how much each would change my confidence in the numbers, not by how interesting it is to build.

1. **Write the escalation policy down before labelling, then re-label against it (~2 h).** My escalation decisions moved 4× between passes because I was reconstructing the threshold per example rather than applying a fixed one. A written policy — *escalate iff it needs authorisation, private account data, or carries legal/safety weight* — turns a judgement call into a checklist. This is now the cheapest large win available, and it directly repairs the result the agent is best at.

2. **A second annotator on 100 examples (~4 h).** Everything in Section 4 traces back to single-annotator ground truth. Real inter-annotator agreement would tell us whether 0.375 is a weak model or a noisy target.
3. **A stronger judge.** A 7–8B model, or two families ensembled with disagreements routed to a human. Also re-run with A/B order swapped to measure position bias directly, which the current single-reply design can't.
4. **Calibrate escalation on held-out data** and publish the full precision-recall curve so a deployer picks their own operating point instead of inheriting mine. The current 0.45 retrieval threshold never fired once (min observed score 0.460) — it's dead code, and I left it rather than retune it against the test set.
5. **Reweight metrics to production distribution** using the retained sampling weights, and report both figures.
6. **Filter cross-brand traffic** — 6% of "Delta" messages are about other airlines and are unanswerable as posed.
