## 1. Problem framing

### What "good" means for this brand

A support agent here is not judged on fluency. It is judged on whether letting it
answer unsupervised is *safe and useful*, which decomposes into three questions:

1. **Does it know what the customer wants?** — intent classification, measured by
   macro-F1 so tail intents cannot be ignored.
2. **Is its reply grounded in what this brand actually does?** — a fluent reply
   inventing a refund policy the brand does not have is worse than a vague one,
   because it commits the brand publicly.
3. **Does it know when to stop?** — escalation. The asymmetry here is the whole
   design: a **missed escalation** (a fraud victim receives a canned reply) costs
   trust and possibly regulatory exposure, while an **over-escalation** costs a
   few minutes of an agent's time. These are not averaged into a single F1
   anywhere in this report.

The operating target is therefore: **high escalation recall, accept the
precision cost, and only auto-handle where precedent clearly supports it.**

### What I chose not to build

- **No chat UI.** Not asked for, and it would consume time that the evaluation
  needs.
- **No multi-turn dialogue.** The agent handles the opening message. Multi-turn
  state is a genuinely harder problem and would have meant a worse evaluation of
  a bigger system.
- **No fine-tuning.** With a 4GB GPU and ~200 labels, a fine-tune would overfit
  and its evaluation would be less trustworthy, not more.
- **No Banking77.** It is retail-banking intent data whose 77 labels describe a
  different resolution structure. Importing it would produce a taxonomy fitting
  neither dataset. (Decision log #6.)
- **No vector database.** Brute-force cosine is sub-millisecond at this scale.
- **No agentic tool-calling loop.** There are no real backend tools to call; a
  simulated one would measure nothing.

Candidate modes to investigate first, based on the design's known weak points:

1. **Intent boundary collapse** — neighbouring intents with the same vocabulary
   but different resolution paths (e.g. billing-dispute vs. account-admin).
   Check the confusion matrix off-diagonal.
2. **Grounded-but-wrong replies** — retrieval returns topically similar threads
   whose *resolution* does not apply, so the reply is on-brand and confidently
   incorrect. The most dangerous mode, because it looks good.
3. **JSON parse failures from the 3B classifier** — rate is logged; these are
   routed to escalation by design, so they inflate escalation rate.
4. **Over-escalation from broad keyword rules** — "refund" appears in many
   routine questions. Cost is precision, deliberately accepted.
5. **DM-punt contamination in the grounding pool** — where the brand's historical
   answer was "DM us", the agent learns to punt rather than resolve.

## 4. What is misleading about my headline number?

*(Mandatory section. Written to be believed, not to reassure.)*

**The evaluation set was built by one person, who also wrote the prompts.**
Self-agreement measures *consistency*, not *correctness*. A systematic
misunderstanding of an intent boundary is invisible to it, and because the same
person wrote the classifier prompt and the labels, the annotator's blind spots
and the system's blind spots are **correlated in the direction that flatters the
system**. A second independent annotator is the single highest-value missing
piece of this deliverable.

**"Resolution" is not observable in this dataset.** `resolved_proxy` means "the
brand replied publicly and did not push to DMs". Twitter never reveals whether
the customer's problem was actually fixed. Every claim about replies being
"grounded in how the brand resolved similar issues" is really grounded in *how
the brand responded*. For DM-punted threads the real resolution happened
somewhere this dataset cannot see. _[pending: punt rate for chosen brand]_

**n is small, and the confidence intervals are wide.** At n≈200 the 95% CI on
accuracy spans roughly ±6 points. Differences smaller than that are not evidence.
This is why system comparisons use a paired bootstrap and why any comparison
whose interval includes zero is reported as unsupported.

**The judge is a 3B model, and its agreement with me is measured, not assumed.**
_[pending: κ]_ If κ falls in the weak band, reply-quality results are downgraded
to directional and the headline rests on intent and escalation, which do not
depend on the judge at all. Length-bias and self-preference probes are reported
alongside — a judge that rewards verbosity produces a ranking that is partly a
length ranking.

**The golden set is deliberately not production-distributed.** Stratification
over-samples rare intents and escalation-worthy cases so those metrics are
measurable at all. The consequence: **aggregate accuracy on this set is not a
production accuracy estimate.** Sampling weights are retained so metrics can be
reweighted, but the headline figure as stated is a stratified-set figure.

**Escalation labels encode my policy, not the brand's.** I decided what warrants
a human. A real deployment would inherit that policy from the support
organisation, and the escalation metrics would move with it.

**One brand, one time period.** Nothing here shows the approach transfers to
another brand, and the taxonomy is explicitly brand-specific by construction.

**Filtering removed real traffic.** Non-English messages, sub-20-character
messages, and multi-customer pile-ons are excluded from sampling. That traffic
exists in production and is disproportionately hard.

## 5. What I'd do next with one more week

Ordered by how much each would change my confidence in the numbers, not by
how interesting it is to build:

1. **A second annotator on 100 examples** (~4 h). Converts self-consistency into
   real inter-annotator agreement and would expose systematic label errors that
   the current design cannot see. Highest value by a wide margin.
2. **A stronger judge.** Even a 7-8B judge, or an ensemble of two families with
   disagreement flagged for human review, should lift κ materially. Also: re-run
   the judge with A/B order swapped to quantify position bias directly.
3. **Calibrate and tune the escalation threshold on a held-out split**, rather
   than using a fixed 0.45. Report the full precision-recall trade-off curve so a
   deployer can pick their own operating point instead of inheriting mine.
4. **Reweight metrics to production distribution** using the retained sampling
   weights, and report both the stratified and reweighted figures.
5. **Test transfer to a second brand** to separate what is method from what is
   this brand's idiosyncrasy.
6. **Human-in-the-loop error review of the grounded-but-wrong mode**, which the
   automated metrics are least able to catch and which carries the most risk.
